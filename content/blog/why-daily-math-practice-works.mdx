Your child understood it perfectly in class. Two days later, they can't remember how to start the problem. This isn't laziness or a lack of ability. It's how human memory works, and it's the main reason short daily math practice beats one long session a week.

> A math skill learned on Monday and not used again until Sunday has to be partly relearned. A skill practised for ten minutes every day doesn't.

## The forgetting curve

In the 1880s, the German psychologist Hermann Ebbinghaus measured how quickly he forgot lists he had memorised. He found that forgetting is steepest soon after learning, in the first hours and days, and then slows down. This pattern, the **forgetting curve**, has been replicated many times since, including a careful modern replication published in 2015 (Murre & Dros).

![Forgetting curve chart showing retention dropping after an online class, then resetting to 100% after each practice session on days 1, 3 and 6, with the curve flattening each time](/images/blog/why-daily-math-practice-works/forgetting-curve.webp "Each practice session pulls a skill back up, and each time it fades more slowly.")

The chart shows two things that matter for parents:

- **Without review, a new skill drops quickly.** The steepest loss happens in the first day or two.
- **Each practice session resets the curve and flattens it.** After a few well-timed reviews, the skill fades far more slowly. What once lasted hours starts to last days, then weeks.

Psychologists call the second effect the **spacing effect**: the same amount of practice, spread out over time, is remembered much better than practice crammed into one sitting.

## What the research shows for math

The spacing effect isn't just a lab curiosity. It has been tested directly on math.

**Spacing.** In a 2007 study by Doug Rohrer and Kelli Taylor, students learned a new type of math problem. One group did all their practice problems in a single session. Another group did the same number of problems, split across two sessions a week apart. On a test one week later, the spaced group scored **74%**, compared with **49%** for the group that crammed. Both groups did the same number of problems. The only difference was the timing.

**Mixing problem types.** In the same paper, a second experiment compared practice sets where problems were grouped by type (all of one kind, then all of the next) with sets where different types were mixed together. During practice, the grouped sets felt easier. But on a test a week later, students who had practised mixed problems scored **63%**, compared with **20%** for students who had practised them grouped: more than three times higher. Mixed practice forces students to work out *which* method fits each problem, which is exactly what tests ask them to do.

**The big picture.** In 2013, John Dunlosky and colleagues reviewed hundreds of studies on ten popular study techniques. Only two were rated "high utility": practice testing and **distributed (spaced) practice**. Rereading notes and highlighting were rated low.

> Spreading practice out is one of the best-supported findings in learning science. Students remember more from the same amount of practice when it is spread out than when it is crammed into one sitting.

## Why math suffers more from forgetting

Forgetting affects every subject, but math is hit harder, for three reasons.

1. **Math is a ladder.** In history, forgetting one era doesn't stop you understanding the next. In math, skills build on each other. If fraction rules or negative numbers fade, the algebra that depends on them falls apart too.
2. **Working memory is small.** We can only hold a few things in mind at once. When basic facts and methods are automatic, working memory is free for the actual problem. When they're rusty, it fills up with small calculations, and that is when slips happen. Researchers call this *cognitive load* (Sweller, 1988).
3. **Knowing isn't the same as doing.** Recognising a worked example in your notes feels like understanding. But math tests ask students to *carry out* a method from memory. Only active practice, solving problems without looking at the answer first, keeps that skill strong.

This is also why many "silly mistakes" aren't careless at all. A dropped negative or a forgotten step often comes from a method that has half faded and is being rebuilt under time pressure.

> Many so-called silly mistakes happen when a half-forgotten method is rebuilt under exam pressure. Regular practice keeps the method automatic, so there's nothing to rebuild.

## What this means at home

You don't need hours. You need regularity. The research supports these principles, and these are the habits we encourage at Strive:

- **Little and often.** Short practice on most days beats one long session at the weekend.
- **Mostly new, some old.** Spend most of the time on the current topic, plus a few problems from last week, last month or last term to keep older skills alive.
- **Mix it up.** Include a few different problem types in each session, so your child has to decide which method to use.
- **Do, don't reread.** Your child should solve problems from a blank page and check answers afterwards, not read through worked examples first.
- **Check it the next day.** If a topic has just been taught, a quick review the next day catches it at the steepest part of the curve.

## Quick answers

**How often should my child practise math?**
Ideally a short session on most days. Spreading the same amount of practice across several days leads to better long-term memory than doing it all at once.

**How long should a daily practice session be?**
Short enough to do consistently. For most children, 10 to 30 minutes is plenty, depending on age. Regularity matters more than length.

**Is cramming before a test useless?**
Cramming can help on a test the next day, but the knowledge fades quickly afterwards. In math, where later topics build on earlier ones, that lost knowledge has to be relearned.
